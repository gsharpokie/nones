Who Are the Nones?

When surveys ask Americans their religion, a growing share answer atheist, agnostic, or “nothing in particular.” Researchers call them the religious “nones.” Make five guesses about who they are and what they believe, then see what the largest U.S. survey of religion found.

The nones are 29% of adults. Most of them aren’t atheists. About two-thirds say their religion is “nothing in particular,” and most of that group believes in God or a universal spirit.

The fuller picture

The rise may have stalled, for now. The unaffiliated share grew from 16% in 2007 to 29% in 2023–24. But Pew’s yearly surveys have found it at about 28% to 31% every year since 2019, and the Christian share has held between 60% and 64%. Pew’s researchers write that time will tell whether this is “the beginning of a lasting shift.”

Why they expect more change anyway. Each new generation has entered adulthood less religious than the one before, and people mostly don’t become more religious as they age. In the General Social Survey, 6% of Americans born in the 1940s or earlier had no religion when surveyed as young adults in the early 1970s. For those born in the early 2000s, surveyed in the early 2020s, it was 36%. For the share to stay flat, Pew notes, today’s young adults would have to become more religious as they age, or something else would need to change.

Not belonging isn’t the same as not believing. Most nones believe in God or a universal spirit, and many believe in a soul or something spiritual beyond the natural world. Sociologist Grace Davie described a similar pattern in Britain as “believing without belonging.” Asking whether people belong to a religion, believe its teachings, or behave religiously (praying, attending services) can give three different answers about how religious a country is.

Surveys don’t agree on the exact number. In 2022, the General Social Survey found 27% unaffiliated, Gallup about 20%, and Pew 31%. The surveys ask the question differently and reach people in different ways, by phone, online, on paper, or in person. Pew changed from phone interviews to online and paper surveys in its newest study because phone response rates had fallen so far. All three agree on the direction: the share with no religion is far higher than it was a generation ago.

Talk about it

  1. Which guess was furthest off? What image of a “none” were you picturing?
  2. Is someone who believes in God but never attends services religious? Is someone who attends every week but isn’t sure God exists? What does your answer say about how to measure religion?
  3. Young adults are much more likely than older adults to be nones. Is that because people become more religious as they age, or because each generation is different? How could researchers tell those apart?
  4. Why might someone who believes in God still answer “nothing in particular” when asked their religion?
  5. Three major surveys gave three different numbers for the same year. Does that mean surveys can’t be trusted? What would you want to know about each survey before choosing one?

About the data

Most figures come from Pew Research Center’s 2023–24 Religious Landscape Study, a survey of 36,908 U.S. adults conducted online, on paper, and by phone from July 2023 to March 2024 (margin of error ±0.8 points). Birth-cohort figures and the 2022 survey comparison come from Pew’s analysis of the General Social Survey and Gallup in the same report.
